Understanding keratoconus

Your cornea is changing shape.
Here's what that means.

Keratoconus is a progressive condition where the cornea — the clear front surface of your eye — gradually thins and bulges into a cone-like shape. This distortion bends light unevenly as it enters your eye, causing blurred or distorted vision that glasses alone can't fully correct.

It usually starts in the teens or twenties and can progress over 10–20 years. The earlier it's detected, the more options you have. In its mildest form, specialty contact lenses may be enough. In more advanced stages, surgical intervention can stabilise the cornea, improve vision, or both.

The most important thing to understand: keratoconus is treatable at every stage. The goal shifts depending on severity — from stopping progression to restoring functional vision — but no stage is hopeless.

Warning signs

Signs you might
have keratoconus

Blurry or distorted vision

Straight lines appear wavy or bent. Faces and text look unclear even with updated glasses.

Frequent prescription changes

Your glasses prescription keeps changing every few months, and each new pair doesn't quite fix things.

Light sensitivity and glare

Halos around lights at night, sensitivity to bright light, and difficulty driving after dark.

Double vision in one eye

Seeing ghost images or multiple outlines of a single object — even with one eye closed.

Eye rubbing habit

Chronic eye rubbing is both a risk factor and a symptom. If you rub your eyes frequently — stop. It accelerates progression.

Young age of onset

Keratoconus typically appears between ages 15–25. If you're a teenager or young adult with worsening vision, ask about corneal screening.

Treatment options

The right treatment depends
on where you are today

Dr. El Naggar assesses each case individually and recommends only what's needed — nothing more. Here are the treatments available at the clinic.

Corneal Cross-Linking (CXL)

The first-line treatment for progressive keratoconus. UV light and riboflavin drops strengthen the collagen bonds in your cornea, halting further thinning. A 30–60 minute outpatient procedure that can stop keratoconus from getting worse.

Stops progression · outpatient

Intracorneal Ring Segments

Tiny arc-shaped implants inserted into the cornea to flatten and regularise its shape. Improves vision and contact lens tolerance in moderate keratoconus. Can be combined with cross-linking for a dual benefit.

Improves vision · reversible

Corneal Transplant

For advanced keratoconus where the cornea is too thin or scarred for other treatments. Dr. El Naggar performs both partial-thickness (DALK) and full-thickness (PKP) transplants depending on the case. Recovery takes longer, but outcomes are excellent.

Advanced cases · long-term solution
Am I a candidate?

Who can benefit from
keratoconus treatment?

You may be a candidate if:

  • You've been diagnosed with keratoconus at any stage
  • Your vision keeps changing despite new glasses
  • You're between 14 and 55 years old
  • You were told your cornea is thinning
  • You have a family member with keratoconus and want screening

The honest truth:

  • Not every case is suitable for every treatment
  • Cross-linking cannot reverse existing damage — only stop further progression
  • Some advanced cases may need a corneal transplant
  • We will never recommend a procedure unless we believe it's right for your eyes

The only way to know is a thorough corneal assessment. That's what the first visit is for.

No — keratoconus very rarely causes complete blindness. It causes progressive vision distortion, which can become severe if untreated, but modern treatments can halt progression and restore functional vision in the vast majority of cases. The key is early detection and treatment before significant damage occurs.
Anaesthetic drops numb the eye completely before the procedure. During cross-linking, you lie still for about 30–60 minutes while UV light is applied. You may feel mild discomfort or watering for 2–3 days afterwards. Most patients describe it as uncomfortable rather than painful — nothing like what they feared. Pain relief drops and medication are provided.
The first 3–5 days involve the most discomfort — light sensitivity, watering, and blurry vision are normal. Most patients return to desk work within a week. Vision may actually appear slightly worse before it stabilises, which is completely normal. Full visual stabilisation can take 3–6 months. The procedure's effect on stopping progression is typically permanent.
Standard LASIK and SMILE Pro are generally not recommended for keratoconus because they thin the cornea further. However, after stabilising with cross-linking, some patients with mild keratoconus may become candidates for specific surface treatments like PRK. Dr. El Naggar will assess whether this is safe for your specific case — many patients achieve good vision with ring segments or specialty contact lenses without needing laser correction.
