Corneal Ulcers & Infectious Keratitis: Urgency & Care

A corneal ulcer is a sight-threatening emergency that requires immediate medical evaluation. Understanding pathogen types, recognizing early warning signs, and initiating targeted treatment are essential to prevent permanent scarring and vision loss.

Medically Reviewed by Dr. Mohamed El-Naggar

Consultant Cornea & Refractive Surgeon | Fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons (FRCS) • Updated September 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Ocular Emergency: Infectious keratitis can progress to tissue melting or perforation within 24 to 48 hours without treatment.
  • Contact Lens Risk: Overnight contact lens wear and improper hygiene are primary risk factors for bacterial and protozoan infections.
  • Microbiological Scrapings: Diagnostic scrapings identify the exact pathogen (bacterial, fungal, viral, or protozoan) to direct therapy.
  • Never Delay Care: Prompt intervention prevents scarring that might otherwise require Phototherapeutic Keratectomy (PTK) or corneal transplantation.

Understanding Infectious Keratitis

Infectious keratitis refers to an inflammation of the cornea caused by invading pathogens that breach the epithelial protective barrier. Left unmanaged, the infection causes epithelial breakdown, stromal cell loss, and inflammatory infiltration, forming a visible white spot or open sore known as a corneal ulcer.

Because the cornea must remain completely clear for optimal vision, any tissue damage or scarring near the central visual axis can permanently reduce eyesight. Immediate evaluation under a slit lamp microscope is vital.

Common Causes & Pathogen Types

Pathogen Types & Clinical Characteristics

Pathogen Category Common Etiology / Risk Factors Typical Clinical Features
Bacterial Keratitis Contact lens wear, corneal trauma, dry eye (e.g., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus) Rapid onset, intense pain, purulent discharge, dense white stromal infiltrate
Fungal Keratitis Trauma involving plant matter or agricultural tools (e.g., Aspergillus, Fusarium) Feathered infiltrate margins, satellite lesions, slower progression, minimal early pain
Acanthamoeba Keratitis Rinsing contact lenses with tap water, swimming or showering in contact lenses Severe pain out of proportion to clinical signs, ring-shaped infiltrate, radial keratoneuritis
Viral Keratitis Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) reactivation, Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) Dendritic epithelial lesions, reduced corneal sensation, recurrent episodes

Symptoms & Emergency Red Flags

If you experience any of the following symptoms, seek urgent evaluation by a corneal specialist immediately:

  • Severe, sharp eye pain or a feeling like a foreign body is stuck in the eye.
  • Marked eye redness with persistent tearing or thick yellow/green discharge.
  • Sudden blurriness or loss of vision.
  • Extreme light sensitivity (photophobia).
  • A visible white, gray, or yellowish spot on the colored part of your eye.

Diagnostic Protocols & Medical Management

Management begins with determining the specific cause of infection:

  1. Corneal Scraping & Culture: Samples collected from the ulcer margin are cultured and stained (Gram stain, KOH wet mount) to identify the pathogen and test antibiotic sensitivities.
  2. Targeted Antimicrobial Drops: High-concentration fortified antibiotic or antifungal drops are applied hourly around the clock during initial treatment.
  3. Inflammation Control: Once the pathogen is controlled, topical anti-inflammatory agents may be carefully introduced to reduce scar formation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a corneal ulcer an eye emergency?

Yes. A corneal ulcer is an absolute ocular emergency. Aggressive infections can cause corneal perforation or permanent scarring within 24 to 48 hours without targeted treatment.

Can contact lens wearers prevent corneal ulcers?

Yes. Avoid sleeping in contact lenses, never expose them to tap water, clean them only with sterile solution, and replace the storage case regularly.
